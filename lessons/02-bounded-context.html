Bounded context: one product, three models

The same word can mean different things in different parts of the system. The bounded context is the border that keeps each meaning reliable.

1 · Hook — a question before any jargon

What does the word "ticket" mean? On a bus, a ticket gets you a seat. At a football match, a ticket gets you in the stadium — and the price is different, the rules are different, and you cannot use one on the other. Same word, two worlds.

Martin Fowler once worked with an electricity company where the word "meter" meant four different things to four different teams. They were not being sloppy — each team genuinely needed its own meaning. The problem was that everyone pretended it was one concept.

By the end of this lesson you will know what a bounded context is, and you will be able to stop the AI from spreading one concept across the whole repository.

3 · The concept in small steps

New words this lesson: 4

  • Bounded context — a border around one model where words have one meaning.
  • Context map — the picture of how contexts relate and where translation happens.
  • Translation — converting one model's words into another's at the border.
  • Polysemy — one word carrying several meanings at once.

Step 1 — A fence around one meaning

In plain words: a bounded context is a fence around one meaning of a word. Inside the fence the word means exactly one thing; outside it can mean something else.

Precise definition: a bounded context is a boundary within which a model and its terms have one reliable, consistent meaning; the same noun may not mean the same thing outside that boundary.

The analogy — countries with borders: each country has its own laws (rules) and its own language (terms). "Ticket" means different things in transport and at a match. At the border you need translation (context maps, anti-corruption layers). Each country can change its own laws without consulting its neighbors.

Where this analogy stops being true

Country borders are fixed on a map and take wars to move. Context boundaries are a design choice: you draw them where the meaning starts to drift, and you can redraw them later. They are also invisible in the filesystem — nothing forces you to respect them except discipline and tests.

Step 2 — Same noun, different models

In plain words: a word is not a thing — it is a meaning that lives inside a context. Copying the same name around does not make it the same concept.

Precise definition: polysemy — one noun carrying several meanings — is normal in business language; a bounded context resolves it by binding each meaning to its own model.

The analogy: departments in a company. Marketing's "Customer" is not Billing's "Customer": one wants to know what you like, the other wants to know what you owe. Same word, different world. In Astralia, "Email" in the Auth context is a user's unique credential (validated, used to sign in). In a future messaging context, "email" would be a message to send. Same noun, different models.

Where this analogy stops being true

Departments have a shared boss who can force a decision. Contexts do not: each context keeps its own model, and the only bridge is explicit translation at the border.

Why is "email" different in Auth?

Step 3 — The context map: draw the borders

In plain words: once you have contexts, you draw them as boxes on a map and note where translation happens between boxes. That picture is the context map.

Precise definition: a context map shows how contexts (or external systems) relate — which ones share data, which ones translate, and where the seams are. It is the cartography of meaning.

The analogy: the customs desk at the border. Each country keeps its own rules; translation happens only at the crossing. Astralia's seam is the Caelus world: CaelusBirthConverter translates birth data into UT terms at the border with an external library.

Where this analogy stops being true

A context map in DDD is also a political map: it records who depends on whom, not just where the lines are. And unlike a country's border, you may have several small contexts inside one single repository — which is exactly Astralia's situation.

4 · Worked example — I do

I will draw the Astralia context map out loud, the way you will draw yours in a minute. Read the folders with me: src/domain/.

"First I look at src/domain/ and I notice three subfolders: auth, birth, and chart. That is my first clue — three groups of files. But folders are not proof, so I inspect what each group imports.

  • "Auth: User.entity.ts, Email.vo.ts, Password.vo.ts — identity, credentials, sessions. One world: who you are."
  • "Birth Data: BirthData.vo.ts, ports for the repository, the UT converter, the geocoder — date, time, coordinates, timezone. Another world: when and where you were born."
  • "Chart: NatalChart.vo.ts and friends, IChartCalculator — planets, houses, aspects. A third world: what the sky said."

"Now the key check: Auth does not import Chart or BirthData — it stays isolated. But Chart is not fully isolated: its port IChartCalculator.ts imports Birth's types (BirthData and the UT converter's data) to describe its input — a real edge worth knowing. The two contexts are still separate models; the port just borrows the other context's vocabulary, and the adapter translates at the boundary."

What I noticed: the same repository contains three models, and nothing forces them to stay separate except the folders and the import rules. That fragility is why we need tests to protect the borders — a preview of Lesson 08.

5 · Guided practice — we do

Three placement questions, with less help each time. Answer before opening the suggestion.

Q1 — worked with me. Where does Email.vo.ts live — Auth, Birth Data, or Chart?

My reasoning: email is a credential: it must be unique and it is used to sign in. That is identity — the Auth world. It cannot be Birth Data (nobody was born with an email) or Chart (the sky does not care). So: Auth.

Answer 1 — check after you try

Auth. Email.vo.ts sits in src/domain/auth/, and semantically it is a credential — identity language, not birth or chart language.

Q2 — half-worked. Where does DrizzleBirthDataRepository.ts belong?

Starter of the reasoning: it persists something. Ask: which domain type does it save — BirthData? Then the storage belongs to the same world as that type…

Answer 2 — check after you try

Birth Data. It saves and loads BirthData records, so it implements the Birth Data model's persistence port. The implementation lives in infrastructure, but the context it serves is Birth Data.

Q3 — your turn to finish. Where does the natal chart calculation belong — the code that turns UT birth data into planets and houses? Look at CalculateChart.ts.

Start: it produces a NatalChart, and its input is birth data already converted to UT. The question is which world owns the output. Complete the last step: since it creates a NatalChart and uses chart value objects, it belongs to the ______ context, even though it reads birth data that was prepared elsewhere.

Answer 3 — check after you try

Chart. What the file produces and owns is a NatalChart; the birth data it receives is an input from a neighboring context. Reading across a border is normal — pretending they are one model is not.

Suggested map (check after you try)
  • Auth: User, Email, Password, IAuthRepository in the domain; SignIn/SignUp/SignOut in application; BetterAuthRepository in infrastructure.
  • Birth Data: BirthData.vo.ts plus its ports (IBirthDataRepository, IBirthToUTConverter, IGeocoder); the use cases SaveBirthData, GetBirthData, DeleteBirthData, SearchCities; Drizzle, Caelus and OpenMeteo adapters.
  • Chart: NatalChart and its supporting VOs/enums, IChartCalculator, CalculateChart, CaelusChartCalculator and CaelusChartMapper.
  • There is no shared "mega-model": Chart does not know Email, Auth does not know NatalChart.

Honest note: CONTEXT.md says that for now it treats the whole application as a "single bounded context for now". This lesson uses the pedagogical lens of three contexts; noticing that discrepancy between the document and the map you draw is precisely the DDD skill of "distrust the docs, inspect the code".

Tangible win

An Astralia context map — the tool with which you will tell the AI which box each thing lives in before it touches a file.

7 · Feedback + wrap-up

You should now be able to say:

  • "Astralia has three models: Auth, Birth Data, and Chart — I can point to each box on my map."
  • "Same noun does not mean same concept: 'Email' belongs to Auth."
  • "The first architectural question is not 'which file?' but 'in which context?'"

Where the country-border analogy breaks (recap): borders are fixed and physical; context boundaries are design choices, invisible in the filesystem, and can be redrawn.

Next lesson: once you have borders, you need a shared language inside them — ubiquitous language (words are architecture).

Spaced question to remember: "Where should a new Email rule go? — Name the context before the file."

AI Drill — try it with the AI

Ask your assistant: "Where should a new Email rule go?" Demand that the answer name a context before a file — for example "in the Auth context, inside Email.vo.ts" — and explain why it must not touch the Birth Data or Chart contexts. If it only answers "in the Email file", it is choosing files, not models.
