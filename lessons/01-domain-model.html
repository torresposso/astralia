The domain model: the map of the problem

Before writing code, you have to name things. This lesson teaches you to distinguish the domain model from the technology that implements it.

1 · Hook — a question before any jargon

You are asked to draw the map of a city you have never visited. What do you put on it? Streets? Buildings? Parks? What do you leave out — every car? every streetlight? every cable under the pavement?

A good map is not a copy of the city. It is a deliberate simplification: it keeps what a traveler needs and drops the noise. Now imagine your product as the city, and your code as the map. Most codebases fail because they draw everything — databases, HTTP routes, libraries — and forget to draw what the business means.

By the end of this lesson you will know what a domain model is, and you will be able to ask the AI: "What business concept or rule does this change represent?"

3 · The concept in small steps

New words this lesson: 4

  • Domain — the problem space the business cares about.
  • Domain model — the code-level map of that problem.
  • Business rule — a rule the business must always obey.
  • Technical mechanism — how something is done (database, HTTP, library), not what it means.

Step 1 — The domain: what the business cares about

In plain words: the domain is the part of the real world your app must understand — not the computer stuff, just the business stuff.

Precise definition: the domain is the problem space that matters to the business: the concepts, rules, and relationships the application must model, independent of the technology used to build it.

The analogy: a bakery's domain is flour, ovens, and recipes. The color of the delivery van is not part of the bakery's domain. Astralia's domain is users, birth data, and natal charts — not Turso, Drizzle, or HTTP.

Where this analogy stops being true

A bakery could become a delivery business later, and then the van becomes part of its world. Domains are not fixed for eternity: they are a choice about what matters now.

What is the domain of Astralia?

Step 2 — The domain model: a deliberate map

In plain words: a domain model is the map of the business written in code: the important concepts, how they relate, and the rules that govern them.

Precise definition: a domain model is a deliberate, simplified representation of the concepts, relationships, and rules of the problem space; it is not a database schema, a framework diagram, or a list of HTTP endpoints.

The analogy — the city map: streets are relationships between things, landmarks are the important objects (your future entities and value objects), traffic laws are business rules. A map hides cars, traffic lights, and wiring — it only keeps what a traveler needs. The domain model hides databases and HTTP the same way.

Where this analogy stops being true

A paper map never changes; a domain model changes every time the business changes a rule. Also, a map is drawn by a cartographer from outside — your model is written by people inside the business, so it can be wrong in ways a map rarely is.

What does a domain model contain?

Step 3 — Business rules: the laws of the map

In plain words: a business rule is a law the business always obeys, no matter what — and the model must enforce it.

Precise definition: a business rule (or invariant) is a constraint that must remain true at a consistency boundary; validating it is part of the model, not of the database or the HTTP layer.

The analogy: traffic laws on the map — a street is one-way, a bridge closes at night. Astralia's rules include: the birth date must be between 1800-01-01 and today; latitude must be between -90 and 90; an hour that never existed (DST spring-forward) is an error.

Where this analogy stops being true

Traffic laws are enforced by police from outside. In code, the rule must be enforced by the model itself — otherwise a future AI change can quietly skip it.

Which is a business rule in Astralia?

4 · Worked example — I do

I will read one real Astralia file out loud and classify what I see into business concept, rule, or technical mechanism. Follow my reasoning; later you will do the same.

Read with me: src/application/birth/SaveBirthData.ts.

"First I look at the class name: SaveBirthData. A verb — an action, not a thing. I will decide what it is later. Next I look at the constructor: it receives a repository and a utConverter, and both are interfaces. That is interesting: this file does not import Drizzle or Caelus directly; it receives capabilities. Now I read the save() method step by step.

  • "Step 1: for updates it verifies existence and ownership — a business rule (only the owner can change their data)."
  • "Step 2: BirthData.create(...) validates all invariants — date, time, coordinates, timezone. Rule. The comment says 'Validate all invariants via the BirthData value object'."
  • "Step 3: utConverter.convert(birthData) — this is a mechanism: the local→UT conversion is done by the Caelus library somewhere else. The rule here is the DST policy: 'nonexistent' is rejected, 'ambiguous' produces a warning."
  • "Step 4: repository.create/update — mechanism: persistence. Step 5: if the saved record has no time, push a 'whole-sign' warning — rule."

What I noticed: the file is a mix — it orchestrates mechanisms but the decisions it makes (existence, DST policy, whole sign) are business meaning. That is exactly the boundary this course teaches: what it means vs how it is done.

5 · Guided practice — we do

Three questions, with less help each time. Answer before opening the suggestion.

Q1 — worked with me. Open src/domain/birth/BirthData.vo.ts. The method create() validates the date range, the coordinates, and the timezone. Question: is create() a concept, a rule, or a mechanism?

My reasoning: it takes plain values and either returns a BirthData or an error. It does not touch a database or an HTTP request. It is enforcing what must be true — that makes it a rule (specifically, the place where invariants live).

Answer 1 — check after you try

Rule. BirthData.create() is the enforcement point of the model's invariants; the error it returns is the law refusing an illegal state.

Q2 — half-worked. Open src/infrastructure/birth/DrizzleBirthDataRepository.ts. It imports a database, builds SQL-style queries with Drizzle, and writes rows. Question: is this concept, rule, or mechanism?

Starter of the reasoning: look at what it imports — the first import is the db client… and then ask yourself: does any business decision happen here, or is it just moving bytes?

Answer 2 — check after you try

Mechanism. It implements persistence (Turso + Drizzle). The business decision — "is this birth data valid?" — already happened before this file ran; this file only stores the result.

Q3 — your turn to finish. Open src/domain/birth/ports/IBirthDataRepository.ts. It is an interface: create, findById, update, delete. Question: is this concept, rule, or mechanism?

Start: the interface itself performs no work — it describes a capability. Now complete the last step: the interface is owned by the domain and uses the domain's own type (BirthData), so the capability is part of the ______, while the implementation in infrastructure is a ______.

Answer 3 — check after you try

The capability contract is part of the model (it speaks the business language: "give me BirthData", "save BirthData"). The implementation — DrizzleBirthDataRepository — is the mechanism. This split is the heart of Lesson 06.

Hints so you don't get it wrong:

  • Concepts: BirthData, the user who owns the data, date/time/place of birth.
  • Rules (invariants): date validation, latitude/longitude, IANA timezone; the DST policy (nonexistent hour → error, ambiguous hour → warning dst-ambiguous); the whole-sign notice when there is no time.
  • Mechanisms: Turso/Drizzle (persistence), HTTP and the API route (src/pages/api/birth-data/create.ts), the caelus-birth package inside CaelusBirthConverter (local → UT conversion).

If something doesn't fit in any column, ask yourself: is this what it means or how it is done? The UT conversion is a mechanism; the rule "the local time must be converted to UT to calculate the chart" is part of the model.

Tangible win

A one-page domain vs mechanism map you can show the AI before asking for any change — proof that you can see the map behind the code.

7 · Feedback + wrap-up

You should now be able to say:

  • "The domain is what the business means; the model is its map in code."
  • "A business rule belongs in the model — I can point to BirthData.create() as evidence."
  • "Drizzle, Turso, HTTP, and Caelus are mechanisms — how it is done, not what it means."

Where the city-map analogy breaks (recap): maps don't change and are drawn from outside; models change with the business and are written by insiders, so they can be wrong. Treat every model with evidence, not faith.

If the AI does not distinguish the two levels, that is exactly the problem DDD solves: the machine mixes the map with the territory.
